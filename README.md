## Overview

Ap (Vedic Sanskrit áp-) is the Vedic and Sanskrit word for water. In its plural form āpas (sometimes reanalysed as singular āpa-), it denotes both water as a substance and the waters as a divine collective. The Apas are addressed as goddesses in several Rigvedic hymns (notably 7.49, 10.9, 10.30, and 10.137) and in the Atharvaveda, where they are credited with purification, fertility, healing, and the removal of sin. As one of the Panchamahabhuta — the five great elements (earth, water, fire, air, space) of classical Hindu philosophy — Ap is a foundational metaphysical category. The Apas are also closely linked to two other Vedic figures: Apām Napāt ('child of the waters'), associated with Agni, and Varuṇa, called Apām Adhipati ('Lord of the Waters'). The word descends from Proto-Indo-European *hxap, surviving in Hindi as āp and Persian as āb.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Vedic Sanskrit áp- 'water', from Proto-Indo-European *hxap 'water'. Cognate with Avestan āp-, Old Persian āpiš, and Hittite ḫapa-.
- **Meaning**: 'Water' as substance; in plural and divinised form, 'the waters' as a group of goddesses.
- **First use**: Rigveda (composed c. 1500–1200 BCE), in hymns to āpas (the waters).

## Theories & Explanations

### Scientific

**Indo-European inheritance** _(Georges Dumézil, Jaan Puhvel, Stephanie W. Jamison)_

Comparative philology argues the Vedic Apas, Avestan Apō and Apąm Napāt, and elements of Roman Neptunus reflect a shared Proto-Indo-European tradition of deified waters.

**Functional deification of essential resource** _(Wendy Doniger, Michael Witzel)_

Mainstream history-of-religions view: deification of the waters reflects the centrality of rivers, monsoon, and ritual ablution in early Indian and Iranian agrarian society.
